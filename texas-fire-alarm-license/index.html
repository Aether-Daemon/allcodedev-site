The Texas Fire Alarm License, accurately

Which exams you sit, what each one is actually built on, which code editions Texas has adopted, and the certification that lets you skip the technical exam entirely.

Last reviewed 5 November 2026 against the State Fire Marshal’s Office exam page and the Texas Register adoption notice. Every figure below links to its source.

What the license is

Fire alarm work in Texas is licensed by the State Fire Marshal’s Office (SFMO), a division of the Texas Department of Insurance — not by TDLR. This matters because several exam-prep sites say TDLR, and some get the question count wrong as a result.

The Fire Alarm Technician (FAL) license is the one most installers and service techs need. The exams are delivered by PSI, and all of them are closed book.

The four fire alarm exams

Each test has 50 questions, true/false or multiple choice, and you need 70% or better to pass. Questions may refer to a drawing, chart, or figure.

ExamNameWhat it is built on
TFM11Fire Alarm Statutes & RulesTexas Insurance Code Chapter 6002 and Texas Administrative Code 34.600 — and only those.
TFM12Fire Alarm TechnicianThe fire alarm sections of the adopted NFPA standards, including NFPA 72 except the Household Fire Alarm Systems chapter. The Technician Qualifying portion also covers NEC (NFPA 70) Articles 760 and 300, plus NFPA 13, 17, 17A, 25 and 90A.
TFM13Fire Alarm ResidentialOnly the Household Fire Alarm Systems chapter of NFPA 72.
TFM14Fire Alarm MonitoringNFPA 72’s supervising station chapter and related chapters, plus UL 827 Central-Station Alarm Services (2008 edition).

Note the split: TFM11 contains no code at all and TFM12 contains almost no statute. They reward completely different studying, and sitting them with one undifferentiated pile of questions is the most common preparation mistake.

Which code editions Texas tests

This is where stale study material does real damage, because a section number that was renumbered between editions is worse than no citation at all.

Texas adopts NFPA 72-2019. The commissioner of insurance adopted amendments to 28 TAC §34.607 in March 2023, updating the NFPA standards for fire alarm and fire detection systems; the adopted list includes NFPA 72-2019, National Fire Alarm and Signaling Code.

NFPA’s own current edition is 2025, and some vendor pages claim Texas is on 2022 or 2025. Those pages are not the rule text. Confirm the edition your local AHJ enforces — local adoption can differ from the state rule, and the state rule is what the exam is written from.

For the electrical side, NEC Article 760 is the fire alarm article and Article 300 covers general wiring methods. If your study material cites Article 760 sections without saying which NEC edition they came from, treat the section numbers as unverified.

You may be able to skip the technical exam

The SFMO notes that the technician exam may be waived if you hold a current ESA Level II Fire Alarm Technician certification, or by passing the NICET Level II test.

That is a real decision worth doing arithmetic on. NICET Level II prep courses run roughly $100–$300 online and up to about $1,500 for in-person classes, and NICET is portable across states. The Texas TFM12 is cheaper and faster but only counts in Texas. If you expect to work outside Texas, the waiver route may be worth more than the shortcut.

Fees

Fees change, and quoting a stale number helps nobody. The SFMO fee schedule lists amounts such as $20 for a duplicate license or a registration update; exam fees are set by PSI and the license fees by the SFMO.

Check the current amounts directly on the SFMO page below before you budget. Any site quoting you an exact total without a date on it is guessing.

How to actually prepare

TFM11 is memorisation of a statute and a rule chapter; it rewards repetition on definitions, exemptions, renewal timing, and the disciplinary and fee provisions. TFM12 is code application; it rewards knowing where a requirement lives, because the exam is closed book and you cannot look it up.

Practise the two separately, and on the code exam insist that the section numbers you study can be verified in the adopted edition. A citation you cannot find in the tested edition is worse than no citation at all.
